## Chapter 1 — Introduction
There is a silent line that separates living from surviving. It does not appear on maps, it is not found in economic manuals, and it is rarely named in everyday conversation. And yet, millions of people cross it every day—not by choice, but by necessity. This book begins precisely in that territory: the space where the foundations of life stop being guaranteed and start being negotiated.

Maslow’s hierarchy taught us that human needs follow an order: first the body, then safety, and then everything that makes us fully human—belonging, esteem, purpose. But what happens when this order breaks? What happens when someone has to sacrifice the basic in order to achieve the minimum level of stability?

This is the central dilemma.

Some people sleep less to keep a job. Some eat poorly to afford rent. Others ignore the signals of their own bodies to maintain a fragile sense of control. In this scenario, physiology—what should be non-negotiable—becomes a form of currency. The body goes into debt so that life can appear, at least on the surface, minimally secure.

The problem is that this trade charges interest.

Living this way is not only exhausting; it is structurally limiting. When the needs at the base are not fully met, everything else in the pyramid becomes unstable. Relationships weaken, identity fragments, the future loses clarity. The person does not stop having dreams—but begins to dream less often, with less energy, and with less internal space to sustain them.

And still, they continue.

They keep trying to get out. They keep believing that at some point, it will be possible to reorganize life in a more dignified way. They keep operating between two realities: immediate survival and a life that has not yet begun.

This book does not come from a comfortable position. It does not observe from the outside. It is born within this tension.

Throughout this chapter, we will explore what it means to live in this state—where the base of the pyramid does not hold, but does not fully collapse either. Where safety is achieved at the cost of the body itself. Where progress exists, but always comes with wear.

Because before we can talk about growth, purpose, or fulfillment, we must confront a more fundamental question:

what happens to a person who has to negotiate their own survival in order to try to live better?

This is where we begin.

## Expansion — Physiology, the primary resource, versus safety
At the most fundamental level, human physiology is not an abstract concept. It is the continuous process that keeps a person alive: breathing, sleeping, eating, regulating temperature, repairing tissues, maintaining energy, and sustaining cognitive function. These are not luxuries. They are the biological conditions that make any form of existence possible.

Without adequate sleep, the brain loses clarity, emotional regulation weakens, and decision making deteriorates. Without proper nutrition, the body begins to ration energy, compromising immunity, strength, and long term health. Without rest, the nervous system remains in a state of stress, gradually eroding resilience. Physiology is not only about survival in the immediate sense. It is the platform that supports thought, identity, relationships, and purpose.

From an existential perspective, physiology is the ground of being. It is the interface between the individual and reality. Every experience, every perception, every intention is filtered through the state of the body. A depleted body cannot sustain a stable sense of self. A neglected physiology cannot support a coherent life.

This is why it can be understood as a form of treasure. Not in a symbolic sense, but in a functional one. It is the most valuable resource a human being possesses, because it is the source from which all other capacities emerge.

However, a resource without protection is vulnerable.

This is where the concept of safety enters. Safety is not secondary. It is the structure that allows physiology to be preserved, restored, and used effectively. Without safety, the body remains exposed to constant threat, whether physical, economic, or psychological. In such conditions, physiology is no longer cultivated. It is consumed.

A treasure left unprotected invites loss. It can be taken, depleted, or destroyed. In the same way, physiology without safety becomes a resource that is continuously extracted, rather than sustained. It is not enough to possess biological capacity. One must also have the conditions to maintain and protect it.

Safety provides continuity. It allows sleep to be uninterrupted, food to be consistent, and the body to enter states of recovery rather than constant alert. It transforms physiology from a fragile asset into a stable foundation.

But in the current global context, this relationship is inverted.

A significant portion of the world’s population is not using safety to protect physiology. Instead, they are using physiology to obtain safety.

People extend their working hours beyond what the body can recover from. They reduce sleep to maintain employment. They compromise nutrition to meet financial demands. They ignore physical and psychological signals in order to remain functional within unstable systems. In doing so, they are effectively spending their primary resource to secure what should have been its protection.

This creates a structural imbalance.

The body, designed to sustain life, becomes a tool for negotiating survival. Energy is no longer invested in growth or stability, but in maintaining a minimal threshold of security. Over time, this leads to cumulative depletion. The individual may appear stable from the outside, but internally, the system is under constant strain.

The consequence is not only physical fatigue, but a limitation of human potential. When physiology is continuously exploited, higher levels of development—emotional, social, intellectual—become difficult to access and even harder to sustain.

In this sense, the modern condition is not defined by the absence of resources, but by their misallocation.

The primary resource of human life is being used to secure the conditions that should exist to protect it. And as long as this inversion persists, a large part of humanity will remain trapped in a cycle where survival consumes the very foundation required for living.

## Expansion — Red, yellow, green and personal fulfillment
Of course, being on the edge of the red line is not the same as living far below it. The distance matters. The intensity of deprivation changes. The level of immediate risk is different. But there is a common thread that connects everyone who exists along this boundary: a constant state of tension.

To live near the red line is to live without margin. There is no real buffer, no stable ground from which to plan, to rest, or to expand. Even when basic needs appear to be temporarily covered, they remain fragile. One unexpected expense, one loss of income, one disruption in routine can push the individual downward. This persistent uncertainty generates a form of stress that is not episodic, but continuous.

And under continuous stress, the upper layers of human development begin to compress.

Social needs—the need for connection, belonging, and meaningful relationships—become difficult to sustain. Time is limited, energy is rationed, and attention is constantly redirected toward maintaining stability. Relationships may still exist, but they are often strained, interrupted, or secondary to survival demands. The “yellow line,” where social life should expand, becomes narrow and inconsistent.

Recognition, or the “green line,” is also affected. The ability to develop skills, pursue excellence, and be acknowledged for one’s contributions requires time, cognitive clarity, and sustained effort. These are precisely the resources that are compromised when physiology is under pressure. As a result, many individuals remain unseen—not because they lack potential, but because they lack the conditions to express it consistently.

And then there is the highest layer: personal fulfillment.

For many, this is not an abstract concept. It is a deeply felt aspiration—the desire to become, to create, to contribute, to live with coherence and meaning. It is the point where identity aligns with action, where effort translates into purpose. But this level requires stability at every layer beneath it. Without that stability, fulfillment becomes something postponed, deferred, or imagined rather than lived.

In the current world, this entire dynamic is strongly tied to material conditions, particularly the presence or absence of financial resources and supportive environments. Money, in this context, is not merely a tool of consumption. It is a stabilizer. It creates distance from the red line. It allows physiology to be protected, safety to be maintained, and higher needs to be pursued.

Similarly, environment plays a critical role. An environment that supports growth—through access to opportunities, social networks, and basic infrastructure—amplifies the ability to move upward. An environment that lacks these elements reinforces the cycle of survival.

This is why the experience of living near the red line is not defined only by what is missing, but by what is constantly being postponed.

Connection is postponed. Recognition is postponed. Fulfillment is postponed.

And over time, postponement becomes a pattern. A life is lived not in expansion, but in suspension—waiting for the moment when stability will finally allow something more to begin.

## Expansion — Economics, work and the scarcity worldview
This condition may also be tied to how we collectively relate to economy and work, and to the limited—often distorted—assumptions embedded in the dominant socio-economic model. At its core lies a persistent belief: that scarcity is necessary, and that the existence of wealth depends on the existence of poverty. In this view, deprivation is not an accident of the system but a functional requirement of it.

This assumption deserves scrutiny.

Modern economies are built on productivity, exchange, and value creation. In principle, they are capable of generating abundance. Technological progress has expanded output, increased efficiency, and reduced the marginal cost of many goods and services. Yet, the distribution of that abundance remains uneven, and access to basic stability is not guaranteed. The issue, therefore, is not only production—it is allocation, structure, and incentives.

Labor, in this context, becomes the primary interface between individuals and the system. For many, work is not merely a means of contribution or self-expression; it is a condition for survival. When access to income is tightly coupled with continuous performance—often under unstable or demanding conditions—the individual is compelled to trade physiological integrity for economic participation. The body becomes an input in a system that rewards output but does not consistently safeguard the conditions required to sustain it.

The notion that “some must have less so that others can have more” reflects a zero-sum framing that does not fully align with the productive capacity of modern economies. While relative differences in income and status may emerge in any system, the persistence of widespread deprivation alongside high aggregate wealth suggests structural inefficiencies and policy choices rather than inevitability.

In addition, prevailing cultural narratives often reinforce this arrangement. Concepts such as merit, productivity, and worth are frequently conflated, leading to the normalization of overexertion and the moralization of scarcity. Individuals may internalize the idea that continuous strain is necessary, or even virtuous, in order to secure stability.

A more precise framing would recognize that sustainable economic systems require not only output, but also human viability. Physiological stability is not external to the economy—it is a prerequisite for its functioning. When large segments of the population operate under chronic strain, the system incurs hidden costs: reduced health, diminished cognitive capacity, lower long-term productivity, and constrained innovation.

Reexamining these assumptions does not imply eliminating markets or incentives. It implies aligning them with a foundational principle: that the primary resource of any economy is human capacity, and that this capacity depends on conditions that allow individuals to exist, recover, and develop without continuously depleting themselves.

Under such a framework, the objective shifts—from extracting maximum output under constraint to sustaining participation without erosion.

## Expansion — Scarcity as self-reproduction
This status quo was shaped under a clear logic of scarcity, and in doing so, it conditions the trajectory of humanity toward that same scarcity. When systems are designed around the assumption that resources are limited and must be tightly competed for, they tend to reproduce that limitation—not only materially, but psychologically and structurally.

Scarcity, in this sense, becomes self-reinforcing. It informs policy, guides economic behavior, and shapes individual decision-making. People learn to operate defensively, prioritizing short-term survival over long-term development. Institutions, in turn, optimize for control and allocation under constraint, rather than expansion and resilience.

The result is a feedback loop: scarcity-driven systems generate outcomes that validate the original assumption of scarcity. Even in contexts where productive capacity has increased, access remains restricted, and stability remains unevenly distributed.

Under these conditions, the future is not merely uncertain—it is pre-configured. A system built on scarcity does not naturally evolve into abundance; it stabilizes around its founding premise.

Breaking this pattern requires more than redistribution. It requires a shift in the underlying model—from managing limitation to enabling sustained human capacity.

## Expansion — Collective health, loss of productive capacity and ambition
What happens when populations living below the red line begin to accumulate higher rates of physical illness and psychological distress, reducing their capacity to work and participate economically?

At scale, the effects are systemic.

Productivity declines, not because of a lack of willingness, but because of diminished physiological and cognitive capacity. Chronic stress impairs attention, decision making, and emotional regulation. Sleep deprivation and poor nutrition compound these effects, increasing the incidence of both mental and physical disorders. As participation weakens, tax bases contract or become unstable, while public expenditures—particularly in health and social support—tend to rise.

Governments respond within existing constraints: adjusting taxes, reallocating budgets, or increasing debt. In periods of global strain, these adjustments can amplify instability. Economic cycles become more volatile when large segments of the population operate with reduced resilience.

A critical threshold emerges when a significant share of working-age adults becomes intermittently or persistently unable to sustain employment. This is not a hypothetical edge case; it is a measurable risk under conditions of prolonged strain. The system then faces a dual pressure: reduced output alongside increased demand for support.

Under these conditions, the idea of a widespread mental health burden—sometimes framed as a “mental pandemic”—moves from speculation to plausible scenario. While the term is metaphorical, the underlying dynamics are observable: rising prevalence of anxiety, depression, burnout, and related disorders, particularly in populations exposed to sustained instability.

Long-term exposure across generations compounds the effect. Early-life stressors, nutritional deficits, and environmental instability can have durable impacts on development, shaping baseline health and future capacity. Over time, this produces cohorts entering adulthood with reduced physiological and psychological reserves.

Behavioral responses also shift. When conventional pathways to stability are constrained, alternative strategies emerge. Some are adaptive; others are not. Informal or illicit activities can increase where formal opportunities are limited, not solely as moral deviation, but as responses to structural pressure.

At the individual level, ambition does not disappear under these conditions. It persists. However, without viable channels for expression, it may be redirected in ways that carry higher risk for both individuals and institutions.

The cumulative effect is a system under strain, where human capacity—its primary resource—is progressively eroded, and where maintaining stability requires increasing effort with diminishing returns.

## Chapter 3 — A Limit to Growth (Socially and Individually)
I come from a family rooted in a very poor region of Brazil, where extreme poverty is not an abstract concept but a daily condition. In many of these places, survival is not guaranteed. Food, safety, stability—these are not constants, but variables that shift depending on circumstance, opportunity, and sometimes pure chance.

Brazil is a country that exposes inequality with unusual clarity. The contrast between wealth and deprivation is not hidden; it is visible, immediate, and often normalized. Within this landscape, a narrative has developed around those who live at the base of society. They are frequently described as people with “small minds,” as lazy, undisciplined, lacking ambition or vision. When not framed through religion, they are often labeled as uneducated, uncivilized, even “wild” by more privileged segments of the population.

This interpretation is not only inaccurate—it is structurally misleading.

In this chapter, we will examine how living below the red line creates real limitations on learning, development, and long-term growth, using Maslow’s hierarchy as a conceptual map. The key point is not that individuals lack capacity, but that their environment systematically constrains the conditions required to develop that capacity.

The lived reality of those below the red line is fundamentally different from that of those who have access to higher levels of the pyramid. When physiological and safety needs are not consistently met, cognitive and emotional resources are redirected toward immediate survival. The brain adapts to prioritize short-term decision-making, threat detection, and resource allocation. Under these conditions, long-term planning, abstract thinking, and sustained focus become more difficult to maintain—not because of lack of intelligence, but because of adaptive necessity.

Despite this, it is common to hear individuals who have always had their basic needs met—often across generations—make statements such as: “If they wanted to, they would do it,” or “It is equally difficult for everyone,” or “It is just a matter of discipline and focus.” These statements assume a shared starting point that does not exist.

Discipline and focus are not innate traits that emerge in isolation. They are developed within environments that allow for repetition, stability, and feedback. A person who grows up with consistent access to food, sleep, education, and safety has the conditions necessary to build these traits over time. A person who grows up navigating instability must first allocate energy to survival, leaving limited capacity for the structured development of such skills.

The difference is not in potential, but in context.

However, the dominant narrative continues to frame the position of the working class at the base as a result of merit rather than circumstance. This idea is often reinforced by individuals who have managed to rise from these conditions through significant personal effort. Their stories are valid, but they are also exceptions that are frequently generalized into universal explanations.

Survivorship bias plays a central role here. Those who succeed become visible, and their success is interpreted as evidence that the system is equally navigable for all. What remains less visible are the structural barriers that prevent the majority from achieving similar outcomes, despite comparable or even greater effort.

The reality is that achieving personal fulfillment when one already has access to physiological stability, social belonging, and, in some cases, recognition, is profoundly different from attempting to reach that same level while still struggling to secure the base.

Both paths require effort. Both demand resilience. But they are not equivalent.

For individuals operating above the red line, effort is often directed toward optimization—improving performance, refining skills, pursuing goals. For those below the line, effort is directed toward stabilization—maintaining access to basic needs, avoiding collapse, navigating uncertainty.

This distinction matters.

To illustrate this, consider two individuals with similar levels of innate ability. One grows up in a stable environment with access to education, nutrition, and supportive social networks. The other grows up in an environment marked by economic instability, inconsistent access to resources, and exposure to chronic stress. Over time, their developmental trajectories diverge—not because one is inherently more capable, but because one has access to the conditions required for sustained growth.

Learning itself becomes constrained. Education, in theory, is a pathway out of poverty. In practice, its effectiveness depends heavily on external conditions. A student who is well-rested, well-nourished, and emotionally supported can engage with material, retain information, and apply knowledge over time. A student who is hungry, sleep-deprived, or preoccupied with family instability faces additional cognitive load that interferes with these processes.

The limitation is not intellectual—it is physiological and environmental.

Maslow’s hierarchy provides a useful framework for understanding this dynamic. At the base are physiological needs, followed by safety, then social belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. The model suggests that higher levels become accessible as lower levels are secured. However, when the base remains unstable, progression is interrupted.

Individuals below the red line are often forced to operate within the lower tiers of the pyramid, repeatedly addressing needs that should, in theory, have already been resolved. This creates a cyclical pattern where energy is continuously redirected toward maintaining the base, preventing consistent engagement with higher levels.

As a result, growth is not only slowed—it is fragmented.

Social development is affected. Building and maintaining relationships requires time, emotional availability, and a degree of stability. When survival demands dominate, these resources are limited. Networks may exist, but they are often shaped by similar constraints, reducing access to opportunities that typically emerge through more stable social structures.

Recognition is also limited. The ability to demonstrate competence, gain visibility, and receive acknowledgment depends on sustained participation in systems that reward such outcomes. When participation is inconsistent due to external pressures, recognition becomes less accessible, regardless of underlying ability.

At the highest level, personal fulfillment remains distant. Not because it is unattainable in principle, but because the pathway toward it is obstructed by unresolved foundational needs.

This leads to a critical misunderstanding.

When individuals from more stable environments evaluate those at the base, they often assess outcomes without accounting for conditions. They observe the absence of achievement and attribute it to lack of effort or character, rather than recognizing the structural limitations that shape behavior.

This misinterpretation reinforces inequality.

It justifies existing hierarchies, reduces empathy, and shifts responsibility entirely onto the individual, ignoring the broader context in which that individual operates.

At the same time, those within the system may internalize these narratives. Repeated exposure to messages that equate position with merit can lead to self-doubt, reduced expectations, and diminished belief in the possibility of change. Over time, this can further constrain behavior, creating a feedback loop that sustains the initial condition.

It is important to clarify that none of this negates the role of personal responsibility. Individuals make choices, and those choices matter. However, the range of available choices is shaped by environment, and the cost associated with each choice varies significantly depending on context.

A decision that appears simple from one position may carry substantial risk from another.

For example, pursuing additional education may require time, financial resources, and stability. For someone above the red line, these requirements may be manageable. For someone below it, the same decision may involve sacrificing income, increasing vulnerability, or exposing themselves to uncertainty that could have immediate consequences.

In this sense, the path to upward mobility is not only longer for those at the base—it is also more precarious.

Every step forward carries the possibility of regression.

This creates a form of constrained ambition. Individuals may still aspire to more, but their strategies are shaped by the need to minimize risk. Opportunities that require long-term investment may be avoided in favor of options that provide immediate, albeit limited, returns.

Over time, this reinforces the perception that those at the base lack vision or ambition, when in reality, they are operating within a rational response to constrained conditions.

Understanding this distinction is essential.

If growth—both social and individual—is to be expanded, the focus cannot remain solely on changing individual behavior. It must also address the structural factors that limit the effectiveness of that behavior.

This includes ensuring stable access to basic needs, creating environments that support learning and development, and reducing the volatility that forces individuals to prioritize short-term survival over long-term growth.

Without these changes, the system will continue to produce similar outcomes, regardless of the effort applied by individuals within it.

The limit to growth, then, is not a fixed boundary defined by human capacity. It is a variable threshold shaped by conditions.

And for those living below the red line, that threshold remains significantly constrained—not by who they are, but by the environment in which they are required to become.

## Chapter 3 continuation — Layered exposure and constrained adaptation
Now extend that same population into conditions of compounded exposure.

Consider family environments shaped by chronic stress, where financial instability translates into tension, conflict, and, in many cases, neglect or abuse. When caregivers themselves have grown within similar constraints, patterns repeat. This is not merely social transmission; it is generational conditioning. Trauma does not reset between generations. It accumulates, adapts, and reappears in new forms.

Access to healthcare—particularly mental healthcare—is often limited or inconsistent. Preventive care is rare. Psychological support is frequently inaccessible, stigmatized, or entirely unknown. Concepts such as emotional regulation, mindfulness, or therapeutic intervention are not integrated into daily life, not because they are rejected, but because they were never introduced as viable tools.

Daily experience, under these conditions, includes repeated exposure to humiliation—subtle and overt. Social interactions may reinforce perceived inferiority. Institutional environments may treat individuals as problems to be managed rather than people to be supported. Over time, these interactions shape internal narratives. Self-perception adjusts to external feedback.

Frustration becomes structural. Aspirations are present, but pathways are constrained. The gap between desired outcomes and accessible options remains wide. This is not occasional disappointment; it is a persistent condition where effort does not reliably translate into progress.

Overlay this with macro-level instability: recurring economic crises, inflation, unemployment cycles. Add exposure to criminal activity in environments where state presence is inconsistent or adversarial. Include experiences of discrimination—racial, social, or geographic—where identity itself becomes a risk factor.

Interactions with law enforcement may not represent protection, but additional uncertainty. Violence, whether direct or ambient, becomes part of the background environment. Safety is conditional.

Now incorporate health variables. Chronic stress correlates with higher incidence of neurological and psychological disorders. Conditions such as anxiety, depression, and trauma-related syndromes emerge not as isolated cases, but as patterned responses. Physical health is also affected—through inadequate nutrition, limited access to care, and sustained physiological strain.

For individuals with disabilities or neurodivergent conditions, the constraints intensify. Systems are rarely designed to accommodate variability. Support structures are limited. The cost of participation increases.

At the center of this configuration is the nervous system.

Under continuous threat and instability, the nervous system shifts into survival mode. This is a biological adaptation. The body prioritizes immediate response: heightened alertness, rapid reaction, energy conservation in non-essential functions. Over time, this state becomes chronic.

A chronically activated survival response affects cognition, emotion, and behavior. Attention narrows. Long-term planning becomes more difficult. Impulse control may weaken under stress. The system is optimized for short-term survival, not long-term development.

Importantly, these responses are functional within context. They are not signs of inherent deficiency. They are adaptive strategies under constraint.

However, when evaluated from outside that context, these adaptations are often misinterpreted. Behaviors shaped by survival conditions may be labeled as lack of discipline, lack of intelligence, or lack of moral structure. Responsibility is assigned at the individual level, while the environmental drivers remain unexamined.

This leads to a consistent pattern of attribution: populations operating under the highest constraints are often held accountable for the limitations those constraints produce.

To understand this segment of society requires a shift in analysis. The question is not why individuals fail to meet external standards, but how persistent exposure to instability reshapes the conditions under which those standards are pursued.

Within this framework, what appears as deficiency is more accurately understood as constrained adaptation.
