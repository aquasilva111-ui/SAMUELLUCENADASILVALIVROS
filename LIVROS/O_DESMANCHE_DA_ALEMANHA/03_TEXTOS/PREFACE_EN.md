# PREFACE — THE GERMANY THAT HAS NOT YET FINISHED EXISTING

What is Germany? The answer seems simple only as long as we do not examine it closely. A territory? A people? A language? A memory? A civilization? A state? A shared will? All these answers are true to some extent, yet none of them, on its own, can contain what we call Germany. Political Germany is relatively recent when compared with the long existence of peoples, cities, kingdoms, cultures, languages, myths, philosophical systems, institutions, and historical experiences that, long before unification, had already produced some sense of belonging to a broader Germanic space. Germany, therefore, existed in certain ways even before it was able to exist fully as a state.

This is precisely where the problem of this book begins. Perhaps a civilization is not a thing. Perhaps it is a relationship between things. Land, memory, language, symbols, institutions, desires, fears, and expectations begin to connect until millions of individuals can recognize something above them and, at the same time, within them. When this happens, a territory ceases to be merely territory. A population ceases to be merely a population. A history ceases to be merely a sequence of events. A collective reality takes shape.

In my system, I call structures of this kind **Reality Conventions**. An isolated belief-idea possesses little power before the world as a whole. But compatible ideas can find in one another the means to survive. They justify, protect, explain, and reinforce one another. Little by little, they cease to function as solitary ideas and begin to form a kind of pack. When this pack becomes large enough, it begins to shape what a population perceives as possible, impossible, legitimate, absurd, desirable, dangerous, or inevitable. A collective reality then begins to take form.

Germany can also be observed in this way. Before becoming a state, it had to become imaginable. Before possessing a common political border, it had to exist in language, memory, culture, circulation, desire, and identification. And when it finally found an architecture capable of concentrating these forces, something extraordinary happened: a region divided for centuries among multiple centers became one of the greatest scientific, industrial, philosophical, and military powers on the planet.

But it is precisely at this point that I observe a disturbing pattern. In my reading of German history, whenever this great mass begins to approach the threshold between **great power and superpower**, some form of fragmentation reappears. Sometimes territorial. Sometimes political. Sometimes institutional. Sometimes economic. Sometimes psychological. The force remains, but its ability to recognize itself as a unity is broken, diverted, or transformed.

I do not present this idea, at the outset, as a conclusion already proven. It is the question that will guide our investigation. Because perhaps the true dismantling of a nation does not occur only when its borders are redrawn. Perhaps it also occurs when its population can no longer imagine certain forms of itself.

A factory can be destroyed and rebuilt. A city can be destroyed and rebuilt. Territories can be lost while a nation continues to exist. But what happens when what has been struck is a population's very relationship with its collective potency?

This is where this book begins to cross an unusual boundary. I do not observe history through only one language. Science can observe material mechanisms. Economics can observe production and circulation. Sociology can observe collective structures. Psychology can observe fear, trauma, identity, and belonging. Philosophy can ask what a nation, a civilization, or a collective will means. Esotericism can search for correspondences. Magic can observe the relationship between symbols, consciousness, will, energy, and reality.

For me, these languages do not need to destroy one another in order to exist. They can be used as different instruments directed toward the same object.

For this reason, throughout this book there will be moments when I speak of the historical and documentable Germany. At other times, I will present geopolitical interpretations. At others, I will approach historical events through a collective-psychological reading. And there will be moments when we deliberately enter magical and symbolic territory. These layers will not be concealed within one another. When we are dealing with history, we will be dealing with history. When we are dealing with interpretation, we will be dealing with interpretation. When we are dealing with my magical-philosophical cosmology, we will be within it. And when we cross the boundary into a reality that does not yet exist, we will be entering **Alternative Reality S-2003**.

S-2003 arises from the way I understand the historical moment in which I myself entered the world. In my cosmology, 2001 represented the opening of a portal toward a new global humanity. The fall of the Twin Towers symbolically marked a profound rupture. New forms of power, fear, surveillance, communication, identity, and global organization began to occupy the space left behind by the previous century. Humanity had crossed through something, but it was still walking between worlds.

For me, 2003 represents the moment when this new reality finally closed around us. It was also the year in which I was born. On its final day. **December 31, 2003.**

That is why I call my alternative realities **S-2003**. The S stands for Samuel. But it is also the marker of a particular portal. If 2003 represents the sealing of a reality, S-2003 represents the possibility of opening other passages from that very same point.

From a very young age, my creative process rarely appeared to me as an isolated idea. It came in systems. Images found concepts. Concepts found structures. Structures found other structures. Sometimes an entire reality seemed to emerge from the need to answer a single question. Writing became the way I found to give matter to these constructions.

It is from this process that my identification with the archetype of the **Modern Magician-Alchemist** also emerges. The alchemy running through these books is not limited to the ancient dream of transforming lead into gold. It appears in the movement between states: **image into concept, concept into system, system into language, language into book, book into object, object into consciousness, consciousness into possibility, possibility into reality.**

*The Dismantling of Germany* is part of this process. This book begins by looking at a Germany that existed. Then it searches for what was broken. Then it asks which forces remained alive within the fragments. And finally, it crosses over into a Germany that does not yet exist.

This passage will not be an attempt to restore the past. I do not seek to resurrect Reichs, old borders, or fantasies of purity. On the contrary: one of the ideas that will run throughout this work is precisely that Germany has always been far more mixed, polycentric, and complex than mythologies of purity have been willing to admit.

The question will be different. What happens when a civilization learns to transform plurality into potency without transforming unity into uniformity? What happens when a people gains access to a collective will without having to surrender that will to a race, a leader, or a small class that declares itself its owner? And what happens when a nation that has learned to fear its own capacity for unity finally manages to distinguish **potency from domination, pride from supremacy, structure from imprisonment, memory from guilt, belonging from exclusion**?

My hypothesis is that there is something in the German experience that extends beyond Germany itself. For centuries, the Germanic peoples faced the problem of living among many centers. Different states. Different cities. Different religions. Different dialects. Different interests. And yet something continued to ask whether those multiple worlds could belong to a larger structure.

Perhaps this problem is a miniature version of a question that all humanity will encounter in the future. When human beings live not only in different countries, but on different worlds — Earth, the Moon, Mars, orbital habitats, and other places we cannot yet even name — what will continue to make all of them one humanity? What will unity mean when we no longer share the same land?

Perhaps this is why, in my cosmology, Germany corresponds to the microcosm of **Aquarius**: network, science, technology, collectivity, plurality, future, and space. The German question, then, ceases to be merely German. It becomes a laboratory.

And perhaps this is precisely why its history so often seems to revolve around the same problem: how can an extraordinary amount of potency be concentrated without allowing that concentration to destroy what made that potency possible in the first place?

This book will be an attempt to follow that question to its ultimate consequences. First, we will cross the land. Then the peoples. Then Prussia. Unification. Industry. Philosophy. The wars. Psychic hunger. Nazism. Destruction. Division. Reunification. Then we will enter the shadow: Black Saturn, Pluto, imprisonment, extraction, shame, the fear of unity.

And then we will search for another form of the same force. Gaia. The Great Mother-Matter. Pink Saturn. The structure that does not imprison, but sustains. The boundary that is not a cell, but a womb. The memory that does not paralyze, but teaches. The unity that does not demand uniformity.

And after Saturn will come Jupiter. Expansion. Not the expansion that conquers. The expansion that multiplies possibilities. And finally, Aquarius. The network. Future humanity. The Via.

In the end, perhaps we will discover that *The Dismantling of Germany* was never merely a book about what was done to Germany. It is also a book about what Germany can still do with itself. And about a much greater question: **when many worlds begin to exist, will we have learned to remain one people without demanding that everyone be the same?**

Germany has not yet finished existing. Perhaps no civilization ever does. Perhaps civilizations merely pass through forms. And some forms are still waiting for us.
